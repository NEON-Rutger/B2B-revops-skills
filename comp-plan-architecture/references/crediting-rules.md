# Crediting and attribution

The mechanic that causes the most damage and gets the least design attention. In a merged or multi-product organization it is the first thing to settle and the last thing anyone wants to discuss.

## Two founding principles

**Pay for persuasion.** Credit belongs to whoever moved the customer, not to whoever was nearby.

**Credit stops when the seller is no longer accountable.** If a rep cannot influence the outcome, they should not be paid on it.

Everything below is implementation of those two.

## What normal looks like

Survey data via Alexander Group and WorldatWork:

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| Companies applying some duplicate crediting | over 68% |
| Companies crediting only one seller | 42% |
| Companies crediting two or more | 58% |
| Companies paying on 130%+ of actual revenue | 16% |
| Companies crediting five or more sellers on one deal | ~5% |
| Advisory guardrail on total credit | ~112% of actual revenue |
| Companies applying full clawback on non-payment | 67% |

Two labels on that table. The percentages are consultant survey data (Alexander Group / WorldatWork assets, undated publications, sample sizes undisclosed); directional, not measured. The ~112% guardrail row is different in kind: it is practitioner guidance on where total credit should cap out, not a surveyed observation, and no published measurement backs it; treat it as a governance heuristic and set your own cap deliberately.

One documented client case reached **160 people compensated on a single deal**. That is what unbounded exception-granting produces over a few years.

## The three failure modes

Name these explicitly when you see them. They are recognisable and they are the reason plans lose legitimacy.

**1. Ransom payments.** Double credit used to buy a colleague's cooperation rather than because they persuaded anyone. Symptom: credit is granted after the fact, in response to a complaint.

**2. Managers negotiating credit.** Credit must be a rule, not a negotiation. The moment it becomes negotiable, the loudest manager wins, and every rep learns that escalation pays better than selling.

**3. Unbounded expansion.** Each individual exception is reasonable. The accumulation is not. Without a rulebook and a total-credit ceiling, the ratchet only turns one way.

## Mechanics to choose between

| Mechanic | How it works | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Double quota, double credit** | Each participant carries full quota and receives full credit | Encourages teaming and makes expectations explicit through quota. Selling cost correlates poorly with productivity, so team size needs separate governance |
| **Credit splits** | Total credit across contributors equals 100% of the sale | Easier to model cost against results, naturally caps team size. Reduced individual recognition can discourage collaboration |
| **Layered credit** | Three or more contributors, total up to 200%, individual capped at 100% | Handles complex teaming without unbounded cost. More to administer and explain |

Worked commission-model examples in circulation: 25% national account manager / 75% local seller, or a premium collaboration model at 75% / 75% for 150% total.

## Overlay and specialist crediting

The decision rests on three variables: deployment model (dedicated versus on-demand), teaming requirement (high collaboration versus individual selling), and offering type (custom solution versus standardized product). The spectrum runs from 100% teaming with 100% double credit to fully individual.

**The operative distinction:**

- **Solution specialists** get full deal credit.
- **Product specialists** get credit only on their assigned products within designated accounts or territories.

## Designing for a multi-product organization

Where multiple product lines persist rather than converging, the coherent structure is usually:

- **Account-owning AE** carries the account number and takes credit on everything sold into the account.
- **Product specialists** carry a product number and take credit on their product only, inside assigned accounts.
- Total credit lands between 100% and 130%, inside the guardrail.

**Use the asymmetry deliberately.** Pay a higher rate on cross-sell of a non-native product than on renewal of a rep's native product. This is the mechanic that actually funds a cross-sell thesis rather than hoping for it. One documented case shifted 30% of corporate metric weighting toward top-line synergy incentives during integration for this reason.

## Scenarios the rulebook must answer

Do not ship a rulebook that leaves any of these to interpretation. Each one becomes a dispute within two quarters otherwise.

1. Two sellers from different legacy entities both worked the account. Who owns it?
2. A customer of entity A buys a product from entity B with no seller from B involved.
3. An account moves territory mid-cycle. Who is credited on a deal that closes after the move?
4. A deal is signed in one period and amended upward in the next.
5. A multi-year deal. Credit on year one ACV or total contract value?
6. A deal sourced entirely by marketing with no outbound effort.
7. A partner-sourced or channel deal.
8. A renewal that required no seller involvement.
9. A renewal that required heavy intervention to save.
10. A house or unassigned account that buys inbound.
11. A rep leaves before the deal closes. Before payment. After payment but before collection.
12. A deal that churns inside the clawback window.
13. A deal where the customer entity spans two territories.
14. An escalation where a manager or executive closed it personally.

## Clawback design

- Claw back only on deals that churn or fail to pay within a defined window, typically 3 to 6 months.
- Claw back the commission amount, never a penalty on top.
- Prorate. A customer churning at month 4 of a 6-month window means clawing back one third.
- Never claw back base salary. In many European jurisdictions attempting to recover paid base salary is unlawful or unenforceable; which protection applies varies by country, so confirm with counsel per jurisdiction rather than assuming.
- Communicate at plan rollout. A clawback that surprises someone destroys more trust than it recovers money.

## The dispute loop

Roughly 22% of reps experience at least one commission dispute a year and around 9% leave over commission errors or disputes (aggregated vendor survey data, directional). Treat disputes as a retention metric, not an admin metric.

The loop that works:

1. Rep raises a dispute through a single defined channel.
2. It is logged with the deal, the claim and the date.
3. Resolved within a fixed SLA, typically ten working days.
4. The resolution and its reasoning are recorded.
5. If the case was novel, the rulebook is amended and the amendment is published.

Step 5 is what stops the same dispute recurring. Most organizations do steps 1 to 4 and skip it.

## Health check for a crediting model

- Is total credit bounded, and is the ceiling written down?
- Can a rep determine, before working a deal, whether they will be credited?
- Is there any scenario where two people both believe in good faith that they own the deal?
- Are exceptions logged, or remembered?
- Does the model survive a rep leaving mid-cycle?
- Does the sum of credit reconcile to actual revenue in the finance system?
