# EBITDA, adjusted EBITDA and the add-back question

## What EBITDA is for

EBITDA strips out financing structure, tax position and accounting depreciation choices to approximate operating cash generation. It exists so that two businesses with different capital structures can be compared.

**Adjusted EBITDA** then strips out whatever management argues is non-recurring. This is where judgement lives, and it is not a neutral calculation.

**Why this matters more in a PE-owned company than anywhere else.** Adjusted EBITDA usually drives three things at once: covenant tests, management bonus outcomes, and the valuation multiple at exit. It therefore receives sustained, structural, entirely predictable optimistic pressure. Nobody is being dishonest. Everyone is arguing in good faith for their own item.

## Add-backs that are usually legitimate

- Genuine one-off transaction and diligence fees on a completed deal
- A discrete, time-boxed restructuring program with a defined end
- A specific litigation settlement
- Costs of a facility closure that will not recur
- One-time systems implementation costs, where the implementation is genuinely one time

## Add-backs to interrogate

| Add-back | The problem with it |
|---|---|
| **Recurring "one-offs"** | Restructuring costs appearing in three or four consecutive years are operating costs wearing a costume |
| **Run-rate synergies not yet realized** | Adding back savings that have not happened yet is a forecast presented as a result |
| **Share-based compensation** | Non-cash, but it is real dilution and a real cost of hiring. Standard to add back, still worth sizing |
| **Pro-forma full-year effect of acquisitions** | Legitimate in an LTM pro-forma view, but it is not what the company earned |
| **Management fees to the sponsor** | Often added back. Reasonable, but note it is a real cash outflow |
| **"Growth investments"** | The most elastic category. Nearly any spend can be framed this way |
| **Bad debt or a large customer loss** | Sometimes genuinely exceptional, sometimes a recurring feature of the customer base |

## The question to ask

**"What is the bridge from unadjusted to adjusted EBITDA, item by item?"**

This is the single most useful question a commercial leader can ask in a finance conversation. It is not adversarial, it is entirely standard, and any competent CFO has the bridge to hand because auditors and lenders ask for it.

**If the bridge is not readily available, that itself is information.**

Follow-ups, once you have it:

1. Which of these items also appeared last year, and the year before?
2. What proportion of adjusted EBITDA is add-backs? Above roughly 15 to 20% of the adjusted figure, the adjusted number is carrying a lot of argument.
3. Which items would a lender accept, and which are internal only? Lenders usually apply a stricter definition, and there may be two different adjusted EBITDA numbers in circulation.

## Covenants, and why they drive behavior

In a leveraged structure, debt agreements typically test a leverage ratio: net debt divided by adjusted EBITDA, measured quarterly on a trailing twelve month basis. Sometimes an interest cover ratio as well.

**The practical consequences for a commercial function:**

- **Headroom is the constraint nobody mentions.** If leverage is close to the covenant, discretionary spend gets frozen at quarter end regardless of the commercial logic. A hiring plan approved in March can evaporate in September without anyone explaining why.
- **The definition of adjusted EBITDA in the credit agreement may differ from the one in the board pack.** Ask which is being quoted.
- **Timing matters more than usual.** Revenue recognized one quarter later can move a trailing twelve month ratio, which is why finance cares about close discipline in a way that can look bureaucratic from the outside.

**The question worth asking once, quietly:** which covenant is tightest, and what is the headroom? Knowing the answer explains most otherwise inexplicable finance behavior.

## EBITDA is not cash

A caution to hold onto, because EBITDA gets used as though it were cash generation.

EBITDA ignores: working capital movements, capital expenditure, capitalized development spend that was real cash out, interest, tax, and the cash cost of anything added back.

A business can grow adjusted EBITDA while burning cash, by growing fast on monthly billing terms with rising DSO and heavy capitalization. Every one of those is defensible individually. Together they produce a number that looks like health and a bank balance that does not.

**When EBITDA and operating cash flow diverge for more than two consecutive periods, ask what changed in working capital.**

## What good disclosure looks like

- Unadjusted and adjusted EBITDA both shown.
- A line-item bridge between them.
- Prior year add-backs shown alongside, so recurrence is visible.
- The credit agreement definition stated where it differs.
- Operating cash flow shown on the same page, not thirty slides later.
